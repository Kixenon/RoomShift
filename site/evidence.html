Deep Technology brief: Test the Change Before You Make It

Where should the fan go?

The brief asks for one small system, two inputs, one constraint, and a trade-off you can’t escape. Ours is a fan in a furnished home office, solved by RoomShift’s own airflow model. Every number on this page comes from that solver and can be rerun with one command.

How the brief maps to RoomShift

System
Air in a 5.2 × 4.0 × 2.7 m room with a desk, sofa, coffee table, lamp, heater and one pedestal fan. The furniture is solid to the air.
Input 1
Fan distance back from the desk edge: 0.6, 1.0, 1.4, 1.8 or 2.2 m, aimed along the desk.
Input 2
Fan setting: the solver’s fan-strength term, from 1.5 to 7.5.
Constraint
The fan stands inside the room, which the editor enforces at the walls, and stays at or below setting 4.5, our stand-in for a quiet, low-power limit.
Outcome 1
Breeze at the desk: mean air speed where a seated person’s head and chest are. Simulated
Outcome 2
Fan cost: the setting itself. A real fan’s power draw rises steeply with speed (the fan affinity laws put it near the cube), and so does its noise. Not simulated
Trade-off
At every distance, each step up in setting bought more breeze, 25 times out of 25. You can’t get more breeze for free. Where the fan stands decides how much breeze each step buys.

30 runs, one answer

Each cell is a separate solve of the same room: five distances times six settings, including “off”, which gives zero everywhere and isn’t drawn. Hover or tab through the cells. Hatched cells break the setting budget.

We’d choose B.

It delivers the same breeze (1.236 against 1.243 m/s, half a percent apart) at three-quarters of the setting. Moved back a metre, the jet has room to spread across the whole desk instead of hitting its near edge. Up close, part of its push is spent before it reaches you. Going further back doesn’t keep helping: at 2.2 m and setting 4.5 the desk gets only 0.73 m/s.

Checking the model against a known jet

Far from the fan, a free round jet slows roughly as one over distance. We ran the fan alone in an empty 6 × 5 m room and read the speed along its axis.

It doesn’t match, and here’s why

The model pushes air along a 1.8 m zone instead of at a blade, so the jet keeps speeding up to . Past that, it slows much faster than 1/x: at 3 m it reads 0.39 m/s, where a free jet would still carry about 1.2. Two seconds of simulated time isn’t long enough for the jet to reach that far and settle.

What we do about it

Run to steady state on the GPU solver, shrink the push zone to the fan’s own depth, then calibrate against the bench. A handheld anemometer reads one real fan at 0.5, 1, 2 and 3 m, and we tune the fan term until the curve passes through those points.

What does hold

  • Air stays close to incompressible: residual divergence of 0.11 s⁻¹ in configuration B, about 1.3% of its velocity gradients (around 8 s⁻¹).
  • Closed walls carry zero flow through them, on every face.
  • An empty, unforced room stays still and at exactly 20 °C.
  • A heater makes a bounded, local rise, and furniture blocks light behind it.

Each of these is an automated test in the repository, part of the 46 that pass.

The bench: measure a real fan

Hold an anemometer (a handheld one, or a phone app with a clip-on sensor) on the fan’s axis at each distance. Type the readings and they plot against the model above. This is the measured case the brief asks the model to reproduce.

So read the experiment as a comparison, not a forecast. The ranking between distances is the useful output. The absolute speeds wait for calibration.

What’s simulated, and what’s left out

Simulated

  • 3D incompressible airflow around solid furniture, with advection, viscosity and pressure projection
  • Heat carried by that air, spreading and relaxing to a 20 °C room, with simple buoyancy
  • Fans as a directional push, heaters as a local warming rate, open windows as outlets

Calculated or assumed

  • Fan cost: the setting, with power and noise rising steeply with speed. Not modelled.
  • Occupied zones: fixed boxes at seated head and chest height
  • Heater output: an estimated °C per second, not watts

Left out

  • Turbulence models and the fan’s real blade and swirl
  • Steady state: this run covers 2 simulated seconds
  • Heat stored in walls and furniture, radiation, sunlight, HVAC
  • Humidity, people’s body heat, and perceived comfort
  • Any calibration against a measured room, so far

We also tried a second outcome, warmth at the sofa with the heater beside it. Two simulated seconds weren’t enough for heat to reach the seat (under 0.03 °C in every run), so we left it out of the trade-off rather than report a non-result as a finding.
